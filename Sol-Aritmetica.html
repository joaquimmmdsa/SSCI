1.1 Divisibility in \(\mathbb{Z}\)

1.1

Let \(a,b\in\mathbb Z\) with \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). Show that \(\gcd(a+b,\,a-b)\in\{1,2\}\).

Solution. Let \(d=\gcd(a+b,\,a-b)\). Then \(d\mid a+b\) and \(d\mid a-b\), hence \[d\mid (a+b)+(a-b)=2a \qquad\text{and}\qquad d\mid (a+b)-(a-b)=2b.\] So \(d\) is a common divisor of \(2a\) and \(2b\): \[d\mid \gcd(2a,2b)=2\gcd(a,b)=2.\] As \(d>0\), we get \(d\in\{1,2\}\). Both cases really occur: \(a=2,\,b=1\) gives \(\gcd(3,1)=1\), while \(a=3,\,b=1\) gives \(\gcd(4,2)=2\). \(\blacksquare\)

1.2

Show that \(\gcd(ma,mb)=m\cdot\gcd(a,b)\) for all \(a,b,m\in\mathbb Z\).

Solution. If \(m=0\) both sides are \(0\). Assume first \(m>0\). For every prime \(p\), let \(v_p(x)\) be the exponent of \(p\) in \(x\) (with \(v_p(0)=+\infty\), which also covers \(a=0\) or \(b=0\)). Using \(\min(\alpha+t,\beta+t)=t+\min(\alpha,\beta)\): \[v_p\bigl(\gcd(ma,mb)\bigr)=\min\bigl(v_p(m)+v_p(a),\,v_p(m)+v_p(b)\bigr) =v_p(m)+\min\bigl(v_p(a),v_p(b)\bigr)=v_p\bigl(m\gcd(a,b)\bigr).\] Two nonnegative integers with the same valuation at every prime are equal, so \(\gcd(ma,mb)=m\gcd(a,b)\) for \(m>0\).

Sign convention (small remark). With the standard convention that gcds are nonnegative, for \(m<0\) the general identity reads \(\gcd(ma,mb)=|m|\cdot\gcd(a,b)\) (gcds only see \(|ma|,|mb|\)); the formula \(m\,\gcd(a,b)\) is then correct as an identity “up to sign”, and is literally true whenever \(m\ge 0\) or when one declares gcds up to sign. \(\blacksquare\)

Bézout alternative. Write \(\gcd(a,b)=ax+by\). Then \(m\gcd(a,b)=(ma)x+(mb)y\), which shows \(m\gcd(a,b)\) is a common divisor of \(ma,mb\) (both positive multiples), and the converse divisibility follows by the same computation with the roles of \((a,b)\) and \((ma,mb)\) swapped; the valuation proof avoids all case distinctions.

1.3

Is \(\gcd(3a-b,\,b-2a)=\gcd(a,b)\) for all \(a,b\in\mathbb Z\)?

Solution. Yes, it is true. Let \(d=\gcd(a,b)\) and \(g=\gcd(3a-b,\,b-2a)\).

  • Since \(d\mid a\) and \(d\mid b\), we have \(d\mid 3a-b\) and \(d\mid b-2a\), so \(d\) is a common divisor of the two numbers and therefore \(d\mid g\).
  • Conversely, \(g\) divides \[(3a-b)+(b-2a)=a \qquad\text{and}\qquad 2(3a-b)+3(b-2a)=(6a-2b)+(3b-6a)=b,\] so \(g\mid a\) and \(g\mid b\), hence \(g\mid d\).

Therefore \(d=g\) (both are positive). Equivalently, the change of pair \((a,b)\mapsto(3a-b,\,b-2a)\) is given by the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}\) of determinant \(3\cdot 1-(-1)(-2)=1\), i.e. it is unimodular, and unimodular transformations preserve the gcd. \(\blacksquare\)

1.4

Compute \(\gcd(28n-5,\,35n-8)\) for \(n\ge 1\).

Solution. Form the combination that cancels \(n\): \[5(28n-5)-4(35n-8)=140n-25-140n+32=7.\] Hence \(\gcd(28n-5,35n-8)\mid 7\), so the gcd is \(1\) or \(7\). But \[28n-5\equiv -5\equiv 2 \pmod 7,\] so \(7\nmid 28n-5\); therefore the gcd cannot be \(7\).

Answer: \(\gcd(28n-5,\,35n-8)=1\) for every \(n\ge 1\).

1.5

Find the gcd and Bézout coefficients of each pair (and check).

Solution.

a) \(1492,\,1776\). Euclidean algorithm: \[1776=1\cdot 1492+284,\quad 1492=5\cdot 284+72,\quad 284=3\cdot 72+68,\quad 72=1\cdot 68+4,\quad 68=17\cdot 4+0.\] So \(\gcd=4\). Back-substitution: \[4=72-68=72-(284-3\cdot72)=4\cdot72-284=4(1492-5\cdot284)-284=4\cdot1492-21\cdot284\] \[=4\cdot1492-21(1776-1492)=25\cdot1492-21\cdot1776.\] Answer: \(\gcd(1492,1776)=4\) and \(25\cdot 1492+(-21)\cdot 1776=4\). Check: \(25\cdot1492=37300\), \(21\cdot1776=37296\), difference \(4\). ✔

b) \(7469,\,2464\). \[7469=3\cdot2464+77,\qquad 2464=32\cdot 77+0 .\] So \(\gcd=77\). Reading the first identity backwards: \[77=1\cdot 7469-3\cdot 2464.\] Answer: \(\gcd(7469,2464)=77\) and \(1\cdot7469+(-3)\cdot2464=77\). Check: \(7469-7392=77\). ✔

c) \(500,\,11312\). \[11312=22\cdot500+312,\quad 500=312+188,\quad 312=188+124,\quad 188=124+64,\] \[124=64+60,\quad 64=60+4,\quad 60=15\cdot4+0 .\] So \(\gcd=4\). Back-substitution: \[4=64-60=2\cdot64-124=2\cdot188-3\cdot124=5\cdot188-3\cdot312=5\cdot500-8\cdot312\] \[=5\cdot500-8(11312-22\cdot500)=181\cdot500-8\cdot11312.\] Answer: \(\gcd(500,11312)=4\) and \(181\cdot500+(-8)\cdot11312=4\). Check: \(90500-90496=4\). ✔

  • \(\operatorname{lcm}(7469,2464)=\dfrac{7469\cdot2464}{77}=97\cdot2464=239008.\)

1.7

Show that \(\dfrac{4n+5}{2n+3}\) is irreducible for every \(n\ge 1\).

Solution. Any common divisor \(d\) of numerator and denominator must divide \[(4n+5)-2(2n+3)=(4n+5)-(4n+6)=-1 .\] Hence \(d=\pm1\) and the fraction is irreducible (it is already in lowest terms, since \(2n+3>0\)). \(\blacksquare\)

1.8

Solve the Diophantine equations.

Solution. Recall: \(ax+by=c\) has integer solutions iff \(\gcd(a,b)\mid c\); if \((x_0,y_0)\) is one solution, all are \(x=x_0+\frac{b}{g}t,\; y=y_0-\frac{a}{g}t\) (\(g=\gcd(a,b)\), \(t\in\mathbb Z\)).

a) \(93x-81y=3\). Dividing by \(3\): \(31x-27y=1\). Euclidean algorithm: \[31=1\cdot27+4,\quad 27=6\cdot4+3,\quad 4=1\cdot3+1 .\] Back-substitution: \(1=4-3=4-(27-6\cdot4)=7\cdot4-27=7(31-27)-27=7\cdot31-8\cdot27\). So \((x_0,y_0)=(7,8)\). From \(31(x-7)=27(y-8)\) and \(\gcd(31,27)=1\) we get \(x-7=27t\): \[\boxed{x=7+27t,\qquad y=8+31t,\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.}\] Check: \(93(7)-81(8)=651-648=3\). ✔

b) \(71x-50y=1\). Euclid: \[71=1\cdot50+21,\quad 50=2\cdot21+8,\quad 21=2\cdot8+5,\quad 8=1\cdot5+3,\quad 5=1\cdot3+2,\quad 3=1\cdot2+1 .\] Back-substitution: \[1=3-2=2\cdot3-5=2\cdot8-3\cdot5=8\cdot8-3\cdot21=8\cdot50-19\cdot21=27\cdot50-19\cdot71 .\] So \(71\cdot(-19)-50\cdot(-27)=1\), i.e. \((x_0,y_0)=(-19,-27)\): \[\boxed{x=-19+50t,\qquad y=-27+71t,\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.}\] Check: \(71(-19)-50(-27)=-1349+1350=1\). ✔

c) \(42x+18y=34\). \(\gcd(42,18)=6\), but \(6\nmid 34\). No integer solutions. \(\blacksquare\)

d) \(283x-1722y=31\). Euclid: \[1722=6\cdot283+24,\quad 283=11\cdot24+19,\quad 24=1\cdot19+5,\quad 19=3\cdot5+4,\quad 5=1\cdot4+1 .\] Back-substitution: \[1=5-4=4\cdot5-19=4\cdot24-5\cdot19=59\cdot24-5\cdot283=59\cdot1722-359\cdot283 .\] So \(1=(-359)\cdot283+59\cdot1722\), i.e. \(283\cdot(-359)-1722\cdot(-59)=1\). Multiplying by \(31\): \[283\cdot(-11129)-1722\cdot(-1829)=31 .\] Reducing the particular solution modulo \(1722\): \(-11129+7\cdot1722=925\), and correspondingly \(y=-1829+7\cdot283=152\): \[\boxed{x=925+1722t,\qquad y=152+283t,\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.}\] Check: \(283\cdot925-1722\cdot152=261775-261744=31\). ✔

1.9

a) Compute \(\gcd(1911,65)\) by the Euclidean algorithm. b) Decide whether \(3822x+65y=26\) has solutions and, if so, describe them all.

Solution.

a) \(1911=29\cdot65+26\); \(\quad 65=2\cdot26+13\); \(\quad 26=2\cdot13+0\). Hence \[\gcd(1911,65)=13 .\] Bézout coefficients: \(13=65-2\cdot26=65-2(1911-29\cdot65)=59\cdot65-2\cdot1911\), that is \[(-2)\cdot1911+59\cdot65=13 .\]

b) \(3822=2\cdot1911\). Since \(\gcd(2,65)=1\), \[\gcd(3822,65)=\gcd(2\cdot1911,65)=\gcd(1911,65)=13 .\] Because \(13\mid 26\), there are solutions. Multiplying the Bézout identity of (a) by \(2\): \[26=(-4)\cdot1911+118\cdot65=(-2)\cdot3822+118\cdot65 .\] So \((x_0,y_0)=(-2,118)\) is a particular solution. From \(3822(x+2)=-65(y-118)\) and \(3822=13\cdot294,\ 65=13\cdot5\), we get \(294(x+2)=-5(y-118)\); since \(\gcd(294,5)=1\), \(x+2=5s\): \[\boxed{x=-2+5s,\qquad y=118-294s,\qquad s\in\mathbb Z.}\] Check for \(s=1\): \((x,y)=(3,-176)\), and \(3822\cdot3+65\cdot(-176)=11466-11440=26\). ✔

1.10

The birthday game: day times 12, month times 31, and only the sum \(S\) is told. Guess the birthday.

Solution. Let \(d\in\{1,\dots,31\}\) be the day and \(m\in\{1,\dots,12\}\) the month. We know only \[S=12d+31m .\]

Uniqueness: if \(12d_1+31m_1=12d_2+31m_2\), then \(12(d_1-d_2)=31(m_2-m_1)\). Thus \(12\mid 31(m_2-m_1)\), and since \(\gcd(12,31)=1\), \(12\mid m_2-m_1\). But \(|m_2-m_1|\le 11<12\), so \(m_1=m_2\), and then \(d_1=d_2\). So the pair \((d,m)\) is uniquely determined by \(S\) (indeed no two birthdays give the same sum).

Recovering the date: reduce the equation modulo \(12\): \(S\equiv 31m\equiv 7m \pmod{12}\). Since \(7\cdot7=49\equiv1\pmod{12}\), \[m\equiv 7S \pmod{12},\] and \(m\) is the representative of this class in \(\{1,\dots,12\}\) (take \(12\) if the residue is \(0\)). Then \[d=\frac{S-31m}{12},\] which is an integer between \(1\) and \(31\) automatically (the possible sums are \(43\le S\le744\)).

Example. For \(S=353\): \(7\cdot353=2471\equiv 11 \pmod{12}\), so \(m=11\) (November) and \(d=(353-341)/12=1\), i.e. the birthday is November 1st. ✔

1.11

Show that if \(ab\) is a perfect square and \(\gcd(a,b)=1\) (the sheet writes \(\gcd(m,n)=1\), a typo), then \(a\) and \(b\) are both perfect squares.

Solution. If \(a=0\) (then \(b=\pm1\)) or \(b=0\), the claim is immediate; assume \(ab\ne0\). Write \(a=\prod_p p^{\alpha_p}\), \(b=\prod_p p^{\beta_p}\). Two facts:

  1. \(\gcd(a,b)=1\) means that for each prime \(p\), at most one of \(\alpha_p,\beta_p\) is positive.
  2. \(ab=c^2\) means \(\alpha_p+\beta_p=v_p(c^2)\) is even for every \(p\).

By (1), for each prime \(p\) one of \(\alpha_p,\beta_p\) is \(0\), so by (2) the other one equals the even number \(\alpha_p+\beta_p\) (up to sign, i.e. both exponents are even). Hence \(a=\prod_p p^{\alpha_p}\) and \(b=\prod_p p^{\beta_p}\) are products of primes with even exponents: \[a=\Bigl(\prod_p p^{\alpha_p/2}\Bigr)^2,\qquad b=\Bigl(\prod_p p^{\beta_p/2}\Bigr)^2\] are both perfect squares. \(\blacksquare\)

1.12

Show that if \(a^2\mid b^2\) then \(a\mid b\).

Solution. Let \(g=\gcd(a,b)\), and write \(a=ga'\), \(b=gb'\) with \(\gcd(a',b')=1\). Then \(a^2\mid b^2\) becomes \(g^2a'^2\mid g^2b'^2\), hence (cancelling \(g^2\ne0\)) \[a'^2\mid b'^2 .\] If \(|a'|>1\), take a prime \(p\mid a'\). Then \(p\mid a'^2\mid b'^2\), so \(p\mid b'\), contradicting \(\gcd(a',b')=1\). Therefore \(a'=\pm1\), i.e. \(a=\pm g\), and so \(a\mid b\). \(\blacksquare\)

Valuation alternative: \(a^2\mid b^2 \Rightarrow 2v_p(a)\le 2v_p(b) \Rightarrow v_p(a)\le v_p(b)\) for all \(p\), i.e. \(a\mid b\).

1.13

Find the smallest \(n\) such that \(n/2\) is a square, \(n/3\) is a cube and \(n/5\) is a fifth power.

Solution. Write \(n=\prod_p p^{e_p}\). The three conditions say, for every prime \(p\):

  • \(n/2\) square: \(e_2-1\) even and \(e_p\) even for \(p\ne2\);
  • \(n/3\) cube: \(e_3-1\) divisible by \(3\) and \(e_p\) divisible by \(3\) for \(p\ne3\);
  • \(n/5\) fifth power: \(e_5-1\) divisible by \(5\) and \(e_p\) divisible by \(5\) for \(p\ne5\).

So, by CRT-type reasoning on each exponent (the smallest nonnegative solution of a congruence):

prime conditions smallest \(e_p\)
\(2\) \(e\equiv1\pmod2,\; e\equiv0\pmod3,\; e\equiv0\pmod5\) \(15\)
\(3\) \(e\equiv1\pmod3,\; e\equiv0\pmod2,\; e\equiv0\pmod5\) \(10\)
\(5\) \(e\equiv1\pmod5,\; e\equiv0\pmod2,\; e\equiv0\pmod3\) \(6\)
\(p\ne2,3,5\) \(e\equiv0\pmod{2,3,5}\) \(0\)

Hence \[\boxed{n=2^{15}\cdot3^{10}\cdot5^{6}=30233088000000 .}\]

Check: \[\frac n2=2^{14}3^{10}5^6=(2^7\,3^5\,5^3)^2=3888000^2,\qquad \frac n3=2^{15}3^{9}5^6=(2^5\,3^3\,5^2)^3=21600^3,\] \[\frac n5=2^{15}3^{10}5^{5}=(2^3\,3^2\,5)^5=360^5. \;✔\]

1.15

Compute in the indicated modular arithmetic.

Solution.

1.16

Prove: \(a\equiv b \pmod m\Rightarrow ka\equiv kb\pmod m\) for every \(k\). Is the converse true? What if \(k\) is prime?

Solution. If \(m\mid a-b\), then \(m\mid k(a-b)=ka-kb\), so \(ka\equiv kb\pmod m\).

The converse is false in general. Counterexample: \(k=2\), \(m=6\), \(a=1\), \(b=4\): \[2\cdot1=2\equiv 2\cdot4=8 \pmod 6,\qquad\text{but}\qquad 1\not\equiv 4 \pmod 6 .\] The cancellation is only valid when \(\gcd(k,m)=1\) (then \(k\) is a unit mod \(m\)). By Bézout, \(ku+mv=1\), so \(a\equiv kua\equiv kub\equiv b\pmod m\).

Being prime is not enough. Take \(k=2\) (prime), \(m=4\), \(a=1\), \(b=3\): \(2\equiv 6\equiv 2\pmod 4\) but \(1\not\equiv3\pmod4\). The correct hypothesis is \(\gcd(k,m)=1\), not primeness of \(k\). \(\blacksquare\)

1.17

Prove: \(a\equiv b \pmod m\Rightarrow a^n\equiv b^n\pmod m\) for every \(n\). Is the converse true?

Solution. For \(n\ge0\) use induction on \(n\): \(n=0\) is \(1\equiv1\); if \(a^n\equiv b^n\), then multiplying by \(a\equiv b\) (exercise 1.16) gives \(a^{n+1}=a\cdot a^n\equiv b\cdot b^n=b^{n+1}\). (For negative exponents one first needs \(a\) invertible; the statement is meant for \(n\ge0\).)

The converse is false. Counterexample: \(m=3\), \(a=1\), \(b=2\) (taking \(n=2\)): \[1^2=1\equiv4=2^2 \pmod 3,\qquad\text{but}\qquad 1\not\equiv2\pmod3 . \qquad\blacksquare\]

1.18

Prove that every odd prime that is a sum of two squares satisfies \(p\equiv1\pmod4\).

Solution. Preliminary note. Stated literally with “even prime” it is false: the only even prime is \(2=1^2+1^2\), and \(2\equiv2\not\equiv1\pmod4\). The true statement (proved here) is for odd primes, with \(2=1^2+1^2\) the single exceptional prime representable as a sum of two squares.

Squares modulo \(4\) are \(0^2\equiv0\) and \(1^2\equiv1\). Hence a sum of two squares is congruent to \[0,\;1 \text{ or } 2 \pmod 4 .\] Let \(p\) be an odd prime with \(p=a^2+b^2\). Since \(p\) is odd, \(a\) and \(b\) have opposite parity; the square of the even one is \(\equiv0\) and of the odd one is \(\equiv1\pmod4\). Therefore \[p=a^2+b^2\equiv1\pmod4 . \qquad\blacksquare\] (Equivalently: no prime \(p\equiv3\pmod4\) is a sum of two squares.) \(\blacksquare\)

1.20

Are \(29\), \(234\), \(512\) invertible modulo \(1170\)? Find the inverses when possible.

Solution. \(1170=2\cdot3^2\cdot5\cdot13\) (so \(\gcd(a,1170)=1\) iff \(a\) is coprime to \(2,3,5,13\)).

  • \(29\): \(29\) is prime, \(29\ne2,3,5,13\), so \(\gcd(29,1170)=1\): invertible. Euclid: \(1170=40\cdot29+10\); \(29=2\cdot10+9\); \(10=1\cdot9+1\). Back-substitution: \[1=10-9=3\cdot10-29=3(1170-40\cdot29)-29=3\cdot1170-121\cdot29 .\] So \(29^{-1}\equiv-121\equiv 1049\pmod{1170}\). Check: \(29\cdot1049=30421=26\cdot1170+1\). ✔
  • \(234\): \(234=2\cdot3^2\cdot13\) and \(1170=5\cdot234\), so \(\gcd(234,1170)=234\ne1\): not invertible.
  • \(512\): \(512=2^9\), so \(\gcd(512,1170)=2\ne1\): not invertible.

1.21

Find all solutions.

Solution.

a) \(31x=1\) in \(\mathbb Z/101\mathbb Z\). Euclid: \(101=3\cdot31+8\); \(31=3\cdot8+7\); \(8=1\cdot7+1\). Back-substitution: \[1=8-7=8-(31-3\cdot8)=4\cdot8-31=4(101-3\cdot31)-31=4\cdot101-13\cdot31 .\] Hence \(-13\cdot31\equiv1\), i.e. \(x\equiv-13\equiv88\pmod{101}\). Check: \(31\cdot88=2728=27\cdot101+1\). ✔ \(\;\boxed{x\equiv88\pmod{101}}\)

b) \(4x\equiv0 \pmod{18}\). \(18\mid4x\iff9\mid x\) (cancel \(2\), coprime to \(9\)). In \(\mathbb Z/18\mathbb Z\): \(x\in\{0,9\}\). \(\;\boxed{x\equiv0 \text{ or } 9 \pmod{18}}\)

c) \(6x\equiv0 \pmod{30}\). \(30\mid6x\iff5\mid x\). In \(\mathbb Z/30\mathbb Z\): \[x\in\{0,5,10,15,20,25\}. \qquad \boxed{x\equiv0 \bmod 5}\]

a) in \(\mathbb Z/17\mathbb Z\): \(\begin{cases} x+3y=15\\ -2x+14y=1\end{cases}\) b) in \(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z\) and \(\mathbb Z/25\mathbb Z\): \(\begin{cases} 3x+2y=0\\ 2x-4y=2\end{cases}\) c) in \(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z\) and \(\mathbb Z/25\mathbb Z\): \(\begin{cases} 3x+2y=0\\ 4x-4y=2\end{cases}\) d) in \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\) and \(\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z\): \(\begin{cases}3x+2y+z=0\\ x-y-4z=2\\ -x+2y+3z=3\end{cases}\)

Solution.

a) Determinant \(\Delta=1\cdot14-3\cdot(-2)=20\equiv3\pmod{17}\), a unit (\(3^{-1}=6\)). Cramer: \[x=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}15&3\\1&14\end{vmatrix}}{\Delta}=\frac{207}{3}\equiv \frac{3}{3}\equiv 3\cdot6=18\equiv1, \qquad y=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}1&15\\-2&1\end{vmatrix}}{\Delta}=\frac{31}{3}\equiv 14\cdot6=84\equiv16 .\] Substitution method (check): \(x=15-3y\Rightarrow -2(15-3y)+14y=1\Rightarrow 20y\equiv31\equiv14\), and \(20\equiv3\), \(y\equiv 14\cdot6=84\equiv16\), \(x=15-48=-33\equiv1\). Check: \(1+3\cdot16=49\equiv15\); \(-2+14\cdot16=222\equiv1\). \[\boxed{(x,y)=(1,16)\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/17\mathbb Z}\]

b) Determinant \(\Delta=3\cdot(-4)-2\cdot2=-16\). In \(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z\): \(\Delta\equiv-16\equiv4\) (unit, \(4^{-1}=4\)). From \(2x=2+4y\): \(x=1+2y\) (\(2^{-1}=3\)). Substituting in the first equation: \(3(1+2y)+2y=0\Rightarrow 8y\equiv-3\equiv2 \Rightarrow 3y\equiv2\Rightarrow y\equiv4\), \(x\equiv1+8\equiv4\). Cramer check: \(x=\frac{-4}{-16}=\frac{1}{4}\equiv4\), \(y=\frac{6}{-16}=\frac{1}{4}\equiv4\). \[\boxed{(x,y)=(4,4)\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z}\]

In \(\mathbb Z/25\mathbb Z\): \(\Delta\equiv9\), \(9^{-1}=14\). Substitution: \(x=1+2y\) (\(2^{-1}=13\)), \(3(1+2y)+2y=0\Rightarrow 8y\equiv-3\equiv22\Rightarrow y\equiv22\cdot22=484\equiv9\) (\(8^{-1}=22\)), hence \(x\equiv1+18=19\). Cramer check: \(x=\frac{-4}{9}=-4\cdot14=-56\equiv19\), \(y=\frac{6}{9}=6\cdot14=84\equiv9\). \[\boxed{(x,y)=(19,9)\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/25\mathbb Z}\]

c) No solutions, in either ring. Add the two equations: \((3x+2y)+(4x-4y)\equiv0+2\), i.e. \(7x-2y\equiv2\); a cleaner elimination: multiply the first equation by \(4\) and the second by \(3\): \[12x+8y\equiv0,\qquad 12x-12y\equiv6 .\] Subtracting: \(20y\equiv-6\).

  • Modulo \(5\): \(20y\equiv0\) but \(-6\equiv4\), so \(0\equiv4\): contradiction (equivalently, modulo \(5\) the two left-hand sides become proportional, \(2x+3y\), with right sides \(0\) and \(1\)).
  • Modulo \(25\): the values of \(20y\) are \(0,5,10,15,20\), never \(-6\equiv19\): contradiction.

d) In \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\) (where \(3\equiv1\), \(2\equiv0\), \(-1\equiv1\), \(-4\equiv0\)) the system becomes \[x+z=0,\qquad x+y=0,\qquad x+z=1,\] and the first and third equations contradict each other: no solutions.

In \(\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z\): from \(x-y-4z=2\) we get \(x=2+y+4z\). Substituting into the first equation: \[3(2+y+4z)+2y+z=6+5y+13z\equiv0 \;\Rightarrow\; 5y+13z\equiv1 .\] Substituting into the third: \(-(2+y+4z)+2y+3z=-2+y-z\equiv3\Rightarrow y-z\equiv5\Rightarrow y=5+z\). Then \[5(5+z)+13z=25+18z\equiv1 \Rightarrow 18z\equiv-24\Rightarrow 4z\equiv4 \Rightarrow z=1,\] so \(y=6\) and \(x=2+6+4=12\equiv5\). Check: \(3\cdot5+2\cdot6+1=28\equiv0\); \(5-6-4=-5\equiv2\); \(-5+12+3=10\equiv3\). ✔ \[\boxed{(x,y,z)=(5,6,1)\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z}\]

1.23

Which matrices are invertible over the indicated rings? Find the inverses (matrices restored from the PDF).

a) \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\) in \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\); b) \(A\) in \(\mathbb Z/10\mathbb Z\); c) \(B=\begin{pmatrix}3&2&1\\1&-1&-4\\-1&2&3\end{pmatrix}\) in \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Z/10\mathbb Z\); d) \(D=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&0\\3&-4&0&1\\0&0&2&-1\\0&3&2&5\end{pmatrix}\) in \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z,\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z,\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z,\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z,\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z,\mathbb Z/55\mathbb Z\).

Solution. Criterion: a square matrix over \(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) is invertible iff \(\det A\) is a unit modulo \(n\), i.e. \(\gcd(\det A,n)=1\) (if \(\det A\) is a unit, \(A^{-1}=(\det A)^{-1}\operatorname{adj}A\); if \(A\) is invertible, \(1=\det(AA^{-1})=\det A\det A^{-1}\) shows \(\det A\) is a unit).

a) \(\det A=1\cdot4-2\cdot3=-2\equiv1\pmod3\): a unit, so \(A\) is invertible in \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\). Moreover \(A\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) and \[\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\pmod3 .\] \[\boxed{A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z}\] Check: \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\equiv I\). ✔

b) \(\det A=-2\), and \(\gcd(2,10)=2\ne1\): not invertible in \(\mathbb Z/10\mathbb Z\). (Indeed over \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\) both rows coincide: \(A\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\).)

c) \(\det B=3\bigl((-1)\cdot3-(-4)\cdot2\bigr)-2\bigl(1\cdot3-(-4)\cdot(-1)\bigr) +1\bigl(1\cdot2-(-1)\cdot(-1)\bigr)=3\cdot5-2\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1=18 .\)

  • In \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\): \(\det B\equiv0\): not invertible.
  • In \(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z\): \(\det B\equiv3\), a unit (\(3^{-1}=2\)). Gauss–Jordan elimination (or \(B^{-1}=2\cdot\operatorname{adj}B\)) gives \[\boxed{B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&2&1\\2&0&1\\2&4&0\end{pmatrix}\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z}\] Check (products mod 5): row 1 of \(B\) times columns: \(3\cdot0+2\cdot2+1\cdot2=6\equiv1\), \(3\cdot2+2\cdot0+1\cdot4=10\equiv0\), \(3\cdot1+2\cdot1+1\cdot0=5\equiv0\); row 2: \(1\cdot0-1\cdot2-4\cdot2=-10\equiv0\), \(1\cdot2-1\cdot0-4\cdot4=-14\equiv1\), \(1\cdot1-1\cdot1-4\cdot0=0\); row 3: \(-1\cdot0+2\cdot2+3\cdot2=10\equiv0\), \(-1\cdot2+2\cdot0+3\cdot4=10\equiv0\), \(-1\cdot1+2\cdot1+3\cdot0=1\). Thus \(B\,B^{-1}=I\). ✔
  • In \(\mathbb Z/10\mathbb Z\): \(\det B=18\), \(\gcd(18,10)=2\ne1\): not invertible.

d) Expanding \(\det D\) along the first row: \[\det D=1\cdot\begin{vmatrix}-4&0&1\\0&2&-1\\3&2&5\end{vmatrix} -2\cdot\begin{vmatrix}3&0&1\\0&2&-1\\0&2&5\end{vmatrix} =\bigl(-4\cdot12-6\bigr)-2\cdot(3\cdot12)=-54-72=-126 .\] So \(\det D=-126=-2\cdot3^2\cdot7\).

  • \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z,\ \mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z,\ \mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z,\ \mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z\): \(\det D\equiv0\) modulo each of \(2,3,7,9\): not invertible in any of these rings.
  • \(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z\): \(\det D\equiv-126\equiv4\), a unit: invertible, and \[\boxed{D^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}4&4&4&1\\1&3&3&2\\1&3&3&0\\2&1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z}\]
  • \(\mathbb Z/55\mathbb Z\) (\(55=5\cdot11\)): \(\gcd(126,55)=1\), so \(D\) is invertible, and \[\boxed{D^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}24&29&14&41\\16&13&48&7\\51&38&48&35\\47&21&40&15\end{pmatrix}\ \text{in }\mathbb Z/55\mathbb Z}\]

In both cases the inverse was obtained by Gauss–Jordan elimination and verified by direct multiplication \(D\,D^{-1}=D^{-1}D=I\) (mod \(5\) and mod \(55\) respectively). ✔

1.3 The Chinese remainder theorem

1.24

(Sun Tsu Suan-Ching) Counted in threes, two remain; in fives, three remain; in sevens, two remain. How many objects?

Solution. We must solve \[x\equiv2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3),\qquad x\equiv3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 5),\qquad x\equiv2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 7).\] The moduli \(3,5,7\) are pairwise coprime, so the solution is unique modulo \(3\cdot5\cdot7=105\). Sequential substitution: \(x=2+3k\); then \(2+3k\equiv3\pmod5\Rightarrow 3k\equiv1\Rightarrow k\equiv2\) (\(3^{-1}=2\)), so \(k=2+5m\) and \(x=8+15m\). Then \(8+15m\equiv2\pmod7\Rightarrow 1+m\equiv2\Rightarrow m\equiv1\), so \(x=23+105t\). Check: \(23=3\cdot7+2\), \(23=5\cdot4+3\), \(23=7\cdot3+2\). ✔ \[\boxed{x\equiv23\pmod{105}}\] (The smallest positive answer is the classical 23.)

Solve the systems of congruences (restored from the PDF).

a) \(x\equiv10\pmod7,\ x\equiv6\pmod9,\ x\equiv1\pmod{10}\). b) \(x\equiv3\pmod4,\ x\equiv1\pmod{15},\ x\equiv2\pmod7\). c) \(3x\equiv2\pmod4,\ 4x\equiv7\pmod{15},\ 8x\equiv-1\pmod7\).

Solution.

a) Moduli \(7,9,10\) pairwise coprime, \(M=630\). Solve \(x\equiv6\pmod9\), \(x\equiv1\pmod{10}\) first: \(x=1+10k\); \(1+10k\equiv6\pmod9\Rightarrow k\equiv5\pmod9\) (as \(10\equiv1\)), so \(x=51+90m\). Impose \(x\equiv10\equiv3\pmod7\): \(51+90m\equiv3\Rightarrow 2-m\equiv3\Rightarrow m\equiv6\pmod7\) (\(90\equiv-1\)), so \(x=51+90\cdot6+630t=591+630t\). Check: \(591\equiv3\pmod7\), \(\equiv6\pmod9\) (\(9\cdot65=585\)), \(\equiv1\pmod{10}\). ✔ \[\boxed{x\equiv591\pmod{630}}\]

b) Moduli \(4,15,7\) pairwise coprime, \(M=420\). Start from \(x\equiv2\pmod7\): \(x=2+7k\). Then \(2+7k\equiv1\pmod{15}\Rightarrow 7k\equiv-1\equiv14\Rightarrow k\equiv14\cdot13=182\equiv2\pmod{15}\) (\(7^{-1}=13\)), so \(x=16+105m\). Impose \(x\equiv3\pmod4\): \(16+105m\equiv m\equiv3\pmod4\), so \(x=16+315+420t=331+420t\). Check: \(331\equiv3\pmod4\), \(\equiv1\pmod{15}\), \(\equiv2\pmod7\) (\(7\cdot47=329\)). ✔ \[\boxed{x\equiv331\pmod{420}}\]

c) Simplify each congruence (multiply by the inverse of the coefficient): \[3x\equiv2\ (4)\Rightarrow x\equiv2\cdot3\equiv2;\qquad 4x\equiv7\ (15)\Rightarrow x\equiv7\cdot4=28\equiv13;\] \[8x\equiv-1\equiv6\ (7)\Rightarrow x\equiv6\quad(8\equiv1).\] So \(x\equiv2\pmod4\), \(x\equiv13\pmod{15}\), \(x\equiv6\pmod7\) (moduli coprime, \(M=420\)). From \(x=6+7k\): \(6+7k\equiv13\pmod{15}\Rightarrow 7k\equiv7\Rightarrow k\equiv1\), so \(x=13+105m\); \(x\equiv2\pmod4\Rightarrow 1+m\equiv2\Rightarrow m\equiv1\), so \(x=118+420t\). Check against the original congruences: \(3\cdot118=354\equiv2\pmod4\); \(4\cdot118=472\equiv7\pmod{15}\); \(8\cdot118=944\equiv-1\pmod7\) (\(944=135\cdot7-1\)). ✔ \[\boxed{x\equiv118\pmod{420}}\]

1.26

Let \(m_1,\dots,m_n>1\) be pairwise coprime and \(M=m_1\cdots m_n\). Prove that \(a\equiv b\pmod M\) iff \(a\equiv b\pmod{m_i}\) for all \(i\). Relation with CRT?

Solution. (\(\Rightarrow\)) If \(M\mid a-b\), then since \(m_i\mid M\) also \(m_i\mid a-b\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose \(m_i\mid a-b\) for every \(i\). We show \(M=m_1\cdots m_n\mid a-b\) by induction on \(n\). For \(n=2\): \(m_1\mid a-b\) and \(m_2\mid a-b\) with \(\gcd(m_1,m_2)=1\) imply \(m_1m_2\mid a-b\). Inductive step: if \(m_1\cdots m_{n-1}\mid a-b\) and \(m_n\mid a-b\), the two factors are coprime, so their product \(M\) divides \(a-b\).

Relation with CRT. The map \[\varphi:\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z\longrightarrow \mathbb Z/m_1\mathbb Z\times\cdots\times\mathbb Z/m_n\mathbb Z, \qquad [a]_M\mapsto([a]_{m_1},\dots,[a]_{m_n})\] is well defined, and this exercise says exactly that \(\varphi\) is injective (two classes with the same image everywhere coincide, i.e. \(\#\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z=M=m_1\cdots m_n\)). Since domain and codomain both have \(M\) elements, injectivity is equivalent to bijectivity — this is one of the standard formulations of the Chinese Remainder Theorem: \(\varphi\) is an isomorphism iff the \(m_i\) are pairwise coprime. \(\blacksquare\)

1.27

Decide whether the maps (restored from the PDF) are bijective: \[f:\mathbb Z/18\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z, \quad [x]_{18}\mapsto([x]_2,[x]_6,[x]_9);\] \[g:\mathbb Z/18\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z, \quad [x]_{18}\mapsto([x]_2,[x]_9).\]

Solution.

\(g\) is bijective. Indeed \(g([x])=g([y])\) means \(x\equiv y\pmod2\) and \(x\equiv y\pmod9\), and since \(\gcd(2,9)=1\) this is equivalent (exercise 1.26) to \(x\equiv y\pmod{18}\). So \(g\) is injective, and both sets have \(18\) elements, hence \(g\) is bijective. This matches the CRT: \(2\) and \(9\) are coprime, and \(2\cdot9=18\).

\(f\) is not bijective. It is injective: \(f([x])=f([y])\) means \(x\equiv y\) modulo \(2\), \(6\) and \(9\), which is equivalent to \(x\equiv y\) modulo their least common multiple \(\operatorname{lcm}(2,6,9)=18\); hence \([x]_{18}=[y]_{18}\). However \(f\) is not surjective: the codomain has \(2\cdot6\cdot9=108\) elements while the domain has \(18\). Explicitly, the triple \((0,1,0)\) is not in the image: \(x\equiv1\pmod6\) forces \(x\) odd, while \(x\equiv0\pmod2\) forces \(x\) even.

This mismatch is exactly what CRT warns about: the theorem needs the moduli to be pairwise coprime, and here \(\gcd(2,6)=2\ne1\), so there is no reason for \(f\) to be onto (and indeed it is not). The projection \(([x]_2,[x]_6)\) alone would be injective into a \(12\)-element set, and for \(f\) the “surplus” is the redundancy: once \([x]_6\) is known, \([x]_2\) is determined.

1.4 Fermat and Euler’s theorems

1.28

a) Find the inverse of \(37\) in \(\mathbb Z/98\mathbb Z\). b) Compute \(37^{55}\cdot53^{13}\pmod{98}\).

Solution.

a) Euclid: \(98=2\cdot37+24\); \(37=1\cdot24+13\); \(24=1\cdot13+11\); \(13=1\cdot11+2\); \(11=5\cdot2+1\). Back-substitution: \[1=11-5\cdot2=11-5(13-11)=6\cdot11-5\cdot13=6(24-13)-5\cdot13=6\cdot24-11\cdot13\] \[=6(37-24)-11\cdot13=17\cdot24-11\cdot37=17(98-2\cdot37)-11\cdot37=17\cdot98-45\cdot37 .\] So \(-45\cdot37\equiv1\), i.e. \[\boxed{37^{-1}\equiv-45\equiv53\pmod{98}}\] Check: \(37\cdot53=1961=20\cdot98+1\). ✔

b) Since \(53\equiv37^{-1}\pmod{98}\), \[37^{55}\cdot53^{13}\equiv37^{55}\cdot37^{-13}=37^{42}\pmod{98}.\] Now \(\varphi(98)=\varphi(2)\varphi(49)=1\cdot42=42\) and \(\gcd(37,98)=1\), so by Euler’s theorem \(37^{42}\equiv1\pmod{98}\): \[\boxed{37^{55}\cdot53^{13}\equiv1\pmod{98}}\]

1.31

Find all \(n\) with \(10^n+3\) a multiple of \(7\).

Solution. Modulo \(7\), \(10\equiv3\), so the condition is \(3^n+3\equiv0\), i.e. \[3^n\equiv4\pmod7 .\] The powers of \(3\) modulo \(7\) are \[3^1=3,\quad 3^2=2,\quad 3^3=6,\quad 3^4=4,\quad 3^5=5,\quad 3^6=1,\] and the sequence is periodic of period \(6\) (by Fermat, \(3^6\equiv1\)). The value \(4\) appears exactly at the exponent \(4\): \[\boxed{n\equiv4\pmod6}\]

1.32

Find all \(n\) with \(4^n+5\) a multiple of \(15\).

Solution. A multiple of \(15\) must in particular be a multiple of \(5\). But modulo \(5\), \[4^n\equiv(-1)^n\equiv\pm1,\qquad\text{so}\qquad 4^n+5\equiv4^n\equiv\pm1\not\equiv0\pmod5 .\] Hence there is no such \(n\). (Indeed \(4^n\bmod15\) equals \(4\) or \(1\) for every \(n\), never the needed \(10\equiv-5\).) \(\blacksquare\)

1.33

a) Fermat: \(2^{12}\equiv1\pmod{13}\). Order \(\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Z/11\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Z/18\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Q\) by decreasing number of invertible elements.

Solution. The invertible elements are the units: \(\mathbb Q^\times=\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}\) (infinitely many); \(\mathbb Z^\times=\{\pm1\}\) (\(2\) elements); and for the finite rings the count is \(\varphi(m)\): \[\varphi(11)=10,\qquad \varphi(18)=\varphi(2)\varphi(9)=6,\qquad \varphi(12)=\varphi(4)\varphi(3)=2\cdot2=4 .\] Hence, in decreasing order: \[\boxed{\mathbb Q\ \succ\ \mathbb Z\ \succ\ \mathbb Z/11\mathbb Z\ \succ\ \mathbb Z/18\mathbb Z\ \succ\ \mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z}\] (with counts \(\infty,\ 2,\ 10,\ 6,\ 4\)). Note \(\mathbb Z/11\mathbb Z\) is a field, so all its nonzero elements are invertible.

1.37

Mersenne numbers \(M_q=2^q-1\). (a) \(M_q\) prime \(\Rightarrow q\) prime. (b) Smallest counterexample to the converse. (c) If \(q\) prime and \(p\mid M_q\) (\(p\) prime) then \(p\equiv1\pmod q\). (d) Decide whether \(M_{13}\), \(M_{17}\) are prime.

Solution.

a) If \(q\) is not prime, write \(q=ab\) with \(1<a,b<q\) (and \(a>1\)). Using the hint with \(x=2^a\): \[M_q=2^{ab}-1=(2^a)^b-1=(2^a-1)\bigl((2^a)^{b-1}+(2^a)^{b-2}+\cdots+2^a+1\bigr).\] Both factors are \(>1\), so \(M_q\) is composite. Taking the contrapositive: \[M_q\text{ prime}\;\Longrightarrow\;q\text{ prime}. \qquad\blacksquare\]

b) The first primes give \(M_2=3\), \(M_3=7\), \(M_5=31\), \(M_7=127\), all prime, but \[\boxed{M_{11}=2^{11}-1=2047=23\cdot89}\] is composite: the converse of (a) fails, with \(q=11\) the smallest possible counterexample. (Note \(23\equiv1\pmod{11}\), as predicted by (c).)

c) Let \(p\) be a prime with \(p\mid 2^q-1\), i.e. \(2^q\equiv1\pmod p\). Then \(p\ne2\) (as \(M_q\) is odd), so \(\gcd(2,p)=1\) and the multiplicative order \(\operatorname{ord}_p(2)\) of \(2\) modulo \(p\) is defined, and it divides \(q\). As \(q\) is prime, \(\operatorname{ord}_p(2)\in\{1,q\}\). The value \(1\) would mean \(2\equiv1\pmod p\), i.e. \(p=1\), impossible. Hence \[\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=q .\] By Fermat’s little theorem \(\operatorname{ord}_p(2)\mid p-1\), so \(q\mid p-1\): \[\boxed{p\equiv1\pmod q}\] (Moreover \(p\) is odd and \(q\) is odd, so in fact \(p\equiv1\pmod{2q}\).)

d) Any prime divisor of \(M_q\) is \(\le\sqrt{M_q}\) and \(\equiv1\pmod q\) (and odd, hence \(\equiv1\pmod{2q}\)), which leaves very few candidates.

  • \(M_{13}=8191\), \(\sqrt{8191}\approx90.5\). Candidates \(p\equiv1\pmod{26}\) below \(90.5\): \(p=53,\ 79\). Check: \(8191=53\cdot154+29\), so \(53\nmid8191\); \(8191=79\cdot103+54\), so \(79\nmid8191\). Therefore \(M_{13}=8191\) is prime.
  • \(M_{17}=131071\), \(\sqrt{131071}\approx362\). Candidates \(p\equiv1\pmod{34}\) below \(362\): \(p=103,\ 137,\ 239,\ 307\) (note \(341=11\cdot31\), not prime). Check: \[131071=103\cdot1272+55,\quad =137\cdot956+99,\quad =239\cdot548+99,\quad =307\cdot426+289,\] so none divides \(M_{17}\). Therefore \(M_{17}=131071\) is prime.

1.38

d) FALSE as stated for a general ring. The correct identity is \((ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}\), and the given one fails in non-commutative rings. In \(M_2(\mathbb Q)\), take \(a=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) (\(a^{-1}=a\)) and \(b=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\) (\(b^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\frac12\end{pmatrix}\)). Then \(ab=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), \((ab)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\\frac12&0\end{pmatrix}\), and \(a^{-1}b^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac12\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\ne(ab)^{-1}\). h) TRUE. If \(a\mid b+c\) and \(a\mid b-c\), then \(a\) divides their sum \(2b\) and their difference \(2c\). As \(a\) is odd, \(\gcd(a,2)=1\), so \(a\mid b\) and \(a\mid c\).

l) TRUE. This is the distributive law relating gcd and lcm, \(\gcd(a,\operatorname{lcm}(b,c))=\operatorname{lcm}(\gcd(a,b),\gcd(a,c))\), valid in any lattice of divisibility.
